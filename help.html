How to use Access BC

Please note: live, step-by-step directions are still in development and aren't yet meant to be relied on as an assistive device. Read more.

Access BC finds accessible walking routes around Boston College, and guides you along them one step at a time. Everything on the map is also available as text, so you can use it with a screen reader and without seeing the map.

Or use the Listen button beside any section. Reading uses your device's voice, at the speed set in the map's Accessibility settings.

Quick start

  1. Type where you're going in Where to?: a building (“McElroy”), something in one (“Career Center”), or a kind of thing (“restroom”). Or choose one of the buttons under it, such as Single-stall restrooms; More… lists everything Access BC can find.
  2. The route appears straight away, from where you are, with how long it takes and whether it's step-free.
  3. Choose Start. Access BC follows your location, reads each step aloud, and finds a new route if you go off it.
  4. Choose End when you're done.

Everything else, such as starting somewhere other than where you are, adding stops, avoiding indoor routes, or limiting slopes, is under Edit trip in the route. Accessibility settings, the map key, campuses, and this help are in the menu (☰).

Getting directions

Routes use paths that have been checked for accessibility, and the indoor accessible routes through buildings. They start and end at accessible entrances.

The route preview shows the time (at a gentle walking pace, so only a guide), the distance, how much of it is indoors, and Step-free when there are no stairs. Under it:

  • Start begins walking directions (see below).
  • Preview the steps one by one lets you go through the directions before you set off, without following your location.
  • About the destination opens its building information.
  • Route details lists each part of the route, with its slope.
  • Edit trip has the start (From), stops, the destination (To), Swap, and the route options.

The back arrow (←) goes back to Where to?. The next trip starts from your location again.

Starting somewhere else. Directions start from your location, like other map apps. To start from a building, open Edit trip and choose it under From. If your location isn't available, or you're off campus, Access BC opens Edit trip and asks you to choose a start. My location can also be the destination: use Swap to get directions back to where you are.

You can also select a building on the map and choose Directions from here or Directions to here.

Route options

In Edit trip. Your choices are remembered on this device.

  • Avoid stairs is on to start with. Turning it off also allows footpaths that haven't been checked for accessibility.
  • Routes through buildings: use them when shorter, prefer them (indoors is where the elevators are), or avoid them (for example after hours, when buildings may be locked).
  • Steepest slope allowed: 1:12 is the steepest an accessible ramp may be; 1:20 is the steepest an accessible walkway should be.

Reading the route

On the map, the route is a thick blue line, dashed where it goes indoors. Orange and red parts are steeper than 1:20 and 1:12, grey is where the slope isn't known, and dotted dark yellow is stairs. The same information is in Route details as text, and the colours are explained in Edit trip.

While a route is showing, the map only shows features along it, and other paths are faded. Turn off Only show map features along the route, in Edit trip, to see everything.

Adding stops

A trip can have up to five stops between the start and the destination, visited in order.

  • In Edit trip, choose Add a stop and pick a building. Use the up and down buttons to change the order, and the remove button to take a stop out.
  • With a route showing, search for a kind of thing (or choose one of the buttons under Where to?): the results are along your route, and each has Add as a stop. It's put in the right place along the route.
  • While walking, choose More, then Add a stop, to find something along your route. The new route starts from where you are, and stops you've already passed are dropped.

Each stop is a step of its own: “Stop at Fulton Hall. When you're ready, continue…”.

Finding something nearby

Type a kind of thing in Where to?, or choose one of the buttons under it. More… spells out everything Access BC can find: vending machines, cafés, dining halls, single-stall restrooms, lactation rooms, printing, indoor and outdoor seating, blue-light emergency phones, shuttle stops, and elevators.

You'll get the nearest five, by walking distance on accessible paths using your route options. Each can be your destination (Directions), a stop on your route (Add as a stop), or shown on the map. At the top of the results you can change what to find, and where to measure from:

  • My location. If your location isn't available, or you're off campus, Access BC says so and takes you to the Near list to choose a building instead.
  • Along my route, for something on the way (the choice when a route is showing).
  • Any building, listed by campus: for planning ahead, or checking routes from off campus. Directions from a result then start at that building, and the building stays chosen for your next search.

Walking directions

Choose Start under a route. The current instruction is at the top of the screen, such as “Turn left and continue for 120 feet, with Gasson Hall on your right”, and the map follows you, facing the way you're walking. At the bottom:

  • Mute stops reading steps aloud (and unmutes).
  • Repeat says the current step again.
  • More has Where am I?, Face the right way, Add a stop, Follow my location (on to start with), Large view, and the Accessibility settings.
  • End stops the directions and goes back to the route.

As you walk, steps move on by themselves, and you're warned shortly before each turn. You can also use Next and Back, the left and right arrow keys, or swipe left and right on the instruction.

  • If you leave the route, Access BC finds a new route from where you are, like other map apps, and carries on: you'll hear “Off route. Finding a new route from here”, then “New route” and the first step. Stops you haven't reached yet are kept. To choose for yourself instead, turn off find a new route automatically in the Accessibility settings; Access BC then tells you which way the route is, and offers a Re-route from here button.
  • Under each instruction you may see cues for that step: curb ramps ahead (often where you cross a street or driveway), stairs that branch off the path, and outdoor seating. The slope of the step is shown too.
  • Reloading the page doesn't lose your trip. The start, stops, destination, and the step you're on are kept in the page's address, so after a reload (or if your browser closes) you're back where you were. Phones only allow speech after you press something, so choose Repeat to hear the current step again. You can also share the address to send someone the same route.

To read through a route before you go, without following your location, choose Preview the steps one by one instead of Start.

Spoken directions

Walking directions are read aloud, unless you've turned on I use a screen reader (your screen reader reads them instead).

  • Mute, at the bottom of the screen while walking, turns reading aloud off; it stays off for next time until you unmute. It's the same setting as Read walking directions aloud in the Accessibility settings, so muting there or with the button does the same thing. Nothing is read aloud until you choose Start.
  • Change the speaking speed in the Accessibility settings, and use Test to hear it.
  • Speech uses your device's own voice; nothing is sent anywhere.

Hands free, phone in your pocket

  • The screen stays on during step-by-step directions, because phones stop updating your location and stop speaking when they lock. Avoid pressing the side button; if the phone does lock, unlock it and guidance carries on.
  • Short sounds mark what's happening, so you don't have to listen to a whole sentence:
    • one tone: a new step. For a turn, it comes from that side (with headphones);
    • two quick high tones: a turn is coming up;
    • two falling tones: you seem to have left the route;
    • three rising tones: you've reached a stop or your destination.
    Turn them off with Play short sounds in the Accessibility settings.
  • Vibration (Android phones): two pulses for a left turn, three for a right turn, long-short-long at a stop or the destination, and one long buzz if you leave the route. You'll feel the same pattern shortly before a turn, as a heads-up. iPhones don't let web pages vibrate.
  • Use headphones or bone-conduction headphones if you can, so you can hear the directions and still hear what's around you.

Facing the right way, and clock directions

On a phone, Access BC can use the compass to tell you which way to go from the way you're facing, as a clock position: 12 o'clock is straight ahead, 3 o'clock is to your right, 6 o'clock is behind you, and 9 o'clock is to your left.

  • When you set off, after a stop, and when you come out of a building, the step says which way to go: “The way to go is at your 2 o'clock.”
  • Face the right way, under More while walking, guides you with sound. Hold your phone flat in front of you, top pointing ahead, and turn slowly. You'll hear a tick in the ear on the side to turn toward (wear headphones to hear the side), getting quicker as you get closer. Two rising tones and “Facing the right way” mean you're there. If you're off the route, it turns you toward the route instead.
  • Where am I? and the things around you also use clock positions.

On an iPhone, you'll be asked to allow access to motion and orientation the first time; choose Allow. If the compass seems wrong, move your phone in a figure of eight to recalibrate it, and step away from metal railings, cars, and steel doors, which upset compasses.

Without a compass (on a computer, or if access isn't allowed), directions use compass points such as “to the northeast” instead. To always use compass points, turn off Give directions as clock positions in the Accessibility settings.

Where am I?

Choose Where am I? under Where to?, or under More while walking. It tells you:

  • the nearest building, how far it is, and which way;
  • during a route, whether you're on it, which way it is if you're not, and what's next;
  • what's around you: the nearest accessible entrances, an elevator, a blue-light emergency phone, a shuttle stop, and outdoor seating, each with a distance and direction.

The summary and the two nearest things are spoken; the full list is shown in the panel. These are straight-line distances, not walking distances.

Building information

Select a building on the map, or choose it in the search box, to open its information. It lists what's in the building, where its accessible entrances are (“Accessible entrance on the north side”), its elevators, and the nearest blue-light phone, shuttle stop and seating. Entrances and elevators that have been reported as not working are marked.

Using a screen reader

Access BC works with VoiceOver (iPhone, iPad and Mac), TalkBack (Android), and NVDA and JAWS (Windows). You don't need the map: search, directions, Find nearby, Where am I? and building information all work as text.

First, turn on “I use a screen reader” in the Accessibility settings. Your screen reader then reads every step, cue and update, and Access BC won't also speak them, so you don't hear everything twice. Sounds and vibration still work.

  • The first thing on the page is a Skip to Where to? link, which goes straight to the search box.
  • Only one thing is on screen at a time: Where to?, the results, the route, or walking directions. Each has a heading; when you choose a destination or a quick search, focus moves to it. The ☰ menu is a navigation landmark.
  • The search box is a combo box: type, then use the down arrow to move through results and Enter to choose one. Choosing a building opens its information.
  • From, To, the stops, and the route options are standard form controls.
  • When a route is found, you'll hear a short summary. The full step list follows the start buttons.
  • While walking, each new step is read automatically, with its cues. After Start, focus is on Repeat; in a preview, on Next.
  • Map layer buttons say whether each layer is shown, and dialogs (Accessibility, About, building information) keep focus inside until you close them with the Close button or Escape.

VoiceOver on iPhone

  • Set the rotor to Headings to jump between sections of the route and of this page.
  • With Follow my location or audio guidance on, steps move on as you walk; you don't need to find the Next button.
  • If you hear steps twice, turn on I use a screen reader in the Accessibility settings.

TalkBack on Android

  • Use the reading controls to move by headings or controls.
  • Android phones can also vibrate at each new step; it's on by default under Accessibility.

Low vision and display settings

Choose Accessibility settings in the menu (☰), or under More while walking. Settings are saved in this browser on this device, so they stay after you reload or come back later (but not in a private browsing window, and not on your other devices).

  • High contrast: darker text and borders, and thicker paths and routes on the map. “Match my device” follows your system's increased contrast setting.
  • Dark mode: a dark map and dark panels, easier on the eyes for many people with light sensitivity. “Match my device” follows your phone or computer's dark mode. This help page follows it too.
  • Larger text: bigger text in the panels, on this help page, and for building names on the map.
  • Bigger map symbols, and thicker paths and routes: makes the map's points, icons and lines easier to see.
  • Large step-by-step view: in step-by-step directions, the current step fills the screen with very large text and buttons. Switch it on and off with Large view, under More while walking.
  • Zooming: you can zoom the page with your browser or phone, up to 400%. Access BC rearranges itself to fit, without scrolling sideways; on a small or zoomed screen the route can take the whole screen.
  • Reduce motion: the map jumps instead of gliding.
  • Step-by-step mode can turn the map to face your direction of travel and show buildings in 3D. Turn either off if you find them confusing.
  • The map key is closed to start with, so it doesn't cover the map. Open it with Layers on the map, or Map layers in the menu.

What Access BC can and can't tell you

Live directions are still being developed. They're being tested, and aren't yet reliable enough to depend on as an assistive device. Use them alongside your usual mobility skills, cane, or guide dog, never instead of them, and proceed with caution.

  • Location is approximate. Phone location is usually accurate to 15 to 30 feet outdoors, worse near tall buildings, and unreliable indoors. Use the landmarks and cues, and Next and Back, rather than relying on exact distances.
  • Slopes are estimates from elevation data, and can miss short ramps and curbs.
  • Things change. Construction, closures, snow, and broken elevators or door openers aren't always shown.
  • Not every building can be routed to yet. Some don't have a mapped accessible entrance connected to the path network.
  • Access BC supports your own orientation and mobility skills, your cane or guide dog, and asking for help; it doesn't replace them.

Feedback and reporting problems

If a route is wrong, an entrance or elevator isn't working, or something in Access BC is hard to use, please tell us with the feedback form. Reports from students who use the app every day are the best way to make it better.
